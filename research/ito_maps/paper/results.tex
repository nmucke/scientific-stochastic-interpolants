The frozen models were evaluated on all 20 held-out trajectories, with three origins per trajectory and 16 ensemble members (Figure~\ref{fig:quality-cost}). One It\^o-map evaluation achieved CRPS 0.27287, compared with 0.28802 for 16 SI steps, while reducing mean ensemble latency from 0.613 to 0.046 seconds ($\szItoOneSISpeed\times$). The paired trajectory-mean CRPS difference was $\szItoOneSIDifference$ with a 95\% bootstrap interval $[\szItoOneSILower,\szItoOneSIUpper]$, a 5.26\% lower point estimate. This supports an improvement over that finite-budget SI reference for this trained model and test set.

Against the more resolved SI reference, eight map evaluations gave CRPS 0.27052 versus 0.26780 at 64 SI steps: a \szItoEightSIPercent\% higher point estimate, with ensemble latency 0.341 versus 2.434 seconds ($\szItoEightSISpeed\times$ lower). The paired difference was $\szItoEightSIDifference$ with interval $[\szItoEightSILower,\szItoEightSIUpper]$. This interval does not establish equivalence: no equivalence margin was declared, and it does not include variation between trained student seeds. Increasing the SI budget to 128 steps changed CRPS to 0.26718, a further 0.23\% reduction; numerical sensitivity in other diagnostics must also be considered.

Similar proper scores do not imply preserved uncertainty. Relative to SI at 64 steps, the eight-step It\^o map reduced RMS ensemble spread by 10.66\%, empirical interval coverage by 5.19 percentage points, and increased the fraction of extreme ranks by 4.54 percentage points. Paired 95\% trajectory-bootstrap intervals for these last two differences were $[-6.03,-4.39]$ and $[3.75,5.32]$ percentage points, respectively. These diagnostics indicate residual underdispersion relative to the more resolved reference (Appendix~\ref{app:seed0-calibration}); the spread deficit grows to 16.05\% against SI at 128 steps. Conversely, the one-step map improves coverage and extreme-rank frequency relative to the coarse 16-step SI sampler, alongside its lower proper scores. The supported tradeoff is therefore close proper forecast scores at substantially lower cost, with reduced uncertainty relative to the more resolved sampler, rather than general distribution or calibration retention.

The deterministic map is a strong comparator. Its one-step CRPS is 0.26656 versus 0.26501 for 64 flow-matching steps, a \szFlowOneFMPercent\% higher point estimate with paired difference $\szFlowOneFMDifference$ and interval $[\szFlowOneFMLower,\szFlowOneFMUpper]$, at $\szFlowOneFMSpeed\times$ lower latency. Its observed one-step score is lower than the It\^o map's. It also has 9.46\% less RMS spread than its teacher, and coverage lower by 4.21 percentage points. Eight flow-map steps give CRPS 0.26905, offering no improvement over the one-step map. Neither map's quality improves monotonically with step count. The experiment supports conditional maps as fast forecast samplers, but does not establish superiority of stochastic over deterministic maps.

\subsection{Development and Feature Ablations}
The two-stage procedure improved one-step validation CRPS from 0.3661 to 0.3047 for the It\^o map and from 0.3088 to 0.2885 for the deterministic flow map. These four-trajectory, eight-member development evaluations are reported separately in Appendix~\ref{app:development}. They guided the method and are not independent confirmation.

Increasing the It\^o representation from $K=4$ to $K=16$ reduced the teacher-supervised validation objective but did not improve one-step validation CRPS (0.3086 versus 0.3047). The recorded selection rule retained $K=4$ before testing, with one checkpoint used for all inference budgets. This exploratory ablation controls checkpoint ancestry and optimization budget, but not identical stochastic minibatch or Brownian streams: constructing different path-projection shapes consumes randomness differently. It does not establish that more Brownian features intrinsically worsen forecasts.

\subsection{Cost of Learning the Maps}
The models have comparable capacity: 12,960,164 parameters for the It\^o map, 12,960,100 for the flow map, and 12,893,796 for each teacher. Recorded seed-zero training-and-validation time is 18.99 minutes for the It\^o student and 18.53 minutes for the flow student across both stages (Appendix~\ref{app:compute}). This is additional distillation cost, excluding teacher pretraining and model/data initialization. Faster sampling therefore trades this upfront cost against the number of future forecast ensembles. The speed ratios above compare measured sampling latency and do not claim an end-to-end training advantage.
